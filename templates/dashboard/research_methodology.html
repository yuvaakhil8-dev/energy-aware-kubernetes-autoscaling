Problem statement

Kubernetes autoscaling (HPA) reacts to CPU utilisation but does not directly account for the energy cost of idle or over-provisioned replicas. This project studies whether an energy-aware elastic scaling policy (EAES) can reduce estimated energy per request while preserving service-level objectives.

Objectives

  • Implement three scaling strategies under identical simulated workloads.
  • Estimate energy from CPU utilisation using a documented, reproducible model.
  • Compare energy, latency, replica count and SLA outcomes.
  • Report limitations honestly, separating simulation from measurement.

Autoscaling strategies

Static

Fixed replica count. Serves as the deterministic baseline; does not adapt to load.

HPA-style

Scale up at 80% CPU, scale down at 30%, with 5% hysteresis and a cooldown. Mirrors Kubernetes HPA feedback control.

EAES

SLA-first controller. Scales up on latency violation or sustained high CPU; scales down only under sustained low CPU with SLA compliant and energy above target.

Energy model

P(util) = P_idle + (P_max − P_idle) · util / 100

E_req = P · Δt / N_req

  • P_idle = 30 W (estimated)
  • P_max = 80 W (estimated)
  • Δt = 15 s metric interval
  • N_req = successful requests in the interval
  • Units: power in W, energy in J, energy per request in J/request

These coefficients are illustrative estimates, not calibrated hardware measurements.

Assumptions and limitations

  • CPU utilisation is simulated; no live Kubernetes cluster is used in the local run.
  • Energy is model-estimated from CPU, not measured with a power meter.
  • Repetitions in the current dataset contain identical values, so variance and confidence intervals cannot be computed.
  • Throughput is not stored per record, so energy-per-request cannot be independently recomputed from the file.
  • Docker Desktop / Kubernetes runtime validation was blocked by a WSL2 error in this environment.
